 I don't know when things changed in Pakistan but at least in Barabanki, India, it was not until the 1980s that things changed. The day of the Maulud or the Milad until then used to be a holiday that involved private prayers and feasting. No decorations of houses; no buntings on shops; no illuminations at night; no public parades. We exchanged gifts of homemade *halw--*that was that. Then one year in the that decade of the Eighties I happened to be in Barabanki on that day and was astounded to see that a mile long stretch of the city only major road had been turned into a spectacle—decorated 'gates', stretches of strings with glitters and buntings hanging from them, and a display of electric lights that turned the night into day for all practical purposes. And that in a town whose Muslim population had not set up any public charity since 1943, when a local propertied gentleman established a public school and named it after its almost-British Deputy Commissioner. I was reminded today of that day and my feelings then. Here are three news reports from the Daily Sahafat of Lucknow. In the first a Shi'ah group asks the district administration of Lucknow to restrain the Sunni group that has turned the birthday of the Prophet into a joint celebration of his first three Caliphs from putting up gates and banners on roads and public buildings and to not use the government buses for a display of their signs. The speaker also claims that the Shi'ahs are the 'only true' inheritors of the Prophet's legacy.
